That blue-green shimmer on beef isn't a chemical reaction — it's actually a physical phenomenon. Beef contains iron, fat, and other compounds. **When the muscle fibers are sliced cleanly across the grain, diffraction can occur, giving the meat a blue-green iridescence.**

A few possible reasons for this:

### 1. **Light Interference (Thin-Film Interference)**

- Under certain lighting, the surface of beef fibers can produce interference effects, making the surface look rainbow-like or blue-green. This happens more often when the meat is cut with clean, aligned fibers — and it's **not** a sign of spoilage.

### 2. **Freezing or Refrigeration**

- When frozen beef thaws, moisture seeps out or the surface oxidizes, which can slightly shift the color. Sometimes reflected light makes it look blue-green under certain angles.

### 3. **Surface Oxidation or Bacterial Growth**

- Myoglobin on the beef surface reacts with oxygen to form **metmyoglobin**, which turns meat darker — usually brown or gray-green. If you see blue-green *and* it smells off or feels slimy, bacteria may be breaking down proteins or fats and producing pigments.
- Spoilage signs: foul odor, sticky surface.

### 4. **Additives or Contamination**

- If the beef has touched equipment or packaging with certain chemicals, unusual color shifts can happen.
- Some unscrupulous vendors may illegally add chemicals to meat, causing abnormal coloration.

---

### How to Tell if It's Spoiled

1. **Smell it**

    Fresh beef has a mild, slightly metallic scent. If it smells sour, rotten, or off in any way, it's gone bad.

2. **Look at the surface**

    If the surface is slimy, sticky, or shows obvious discoloration (dark green patches, mold spots), don't eat it.

3. **Press it**

    Fresh beef is springy — press it and it bounces back. If a dent stays, it's likely spoiled.

---

Bottom line: if the blue-green effect is purely from light interference or freezing, the meat is still safe. But if it comes with off smells or other red flags, better play it safe.
