A future is a financial derivative — a standardized contract where a buyer and seller agree to trade a specific underlying asset at a predetermined price on a set future date. The underlying asset can be a physical commodity (gold, crude oil, agricultural products) or a financial instrument (stock indices, interest rates, foreign exchange). Futures trading happens on exchanges with standardized contracts and strict rules.

### Key Features of Futures

1. **Standardized Contracts**

    Futures contracts specify the type, quantity, delivery time, and location of the underlying asset, making trading efficient and regulated.

2. **Leverage**

    Futures trading typically requires only a small margin deposit (usually 5%–15% of the contract value) to control the full contract — a classic "small stake, big outcome" setup.

3. **Dual-Directional Trading**

    You can go long (buy to open) and profit from rising prices, or go short (sell to open) and profit from falling prices.

4. **Expiration and Delivery**

    If you hold a contract to expiration, you must settle via physical delivery or cash settlement. But most futures positions are closed out before expiration through offsetting trades.

5. **Price Volatility Risk**

    Because of leverage, price swings amplify both gains and losses — futures carry substantial risk.

### Main Uses of Futures

1. **Hedging**

    Companies and individuals use the futures market to offset price risk in the spot market. For example, a farmer can lock in a future selling price for their crop via futures.

2. **Speculation**

    Traders profit by predicting price movements — buying low and selling high (or vice versa).

3. **Price Discovery**

    The futures market aggregates expectations about the future price of a commodity or financial asset, providing valuable reference points.

4. **Portfolio Allocation**

    Institutional investors use futures as part of their portfolio to diversify risk or enhance returns.

### A Simple Example

Suppose a wheat farmer expects prices to drop in a few months. They can sell wheat futures contracts now, locking in a future selling price. If prices do fall, the loss on the spot sale is offset by gains in the futures position — stabilizing overall income.

Futures trading has a high barrier to entry and suits investors with sufficient risk tolerance and experience.
